Pre-order for cancer and rare-disease medications

Nhà thuốc An Khang | 2024

Problem

In Vietnam, patients with cancer or rare diseases struggle to find their medication, and often have to buy through wholesale channels of unknown origin. An Khang wanted to solve this by opening a legitimate channel that gives customers access to medication from a clearly identified brand, with a trustworthy origin and transparent pricing. This project reached me as nothing more than a business idea.

Role

I brought sales, customer service, logistics and development together to define a standard end-to-end flow for the feature. I also took on the research and UI design myself, wrote the specs, and ran the kickoff and post-go-live review meetings.

Approach

I started by listening: interviewing the sales team and patients, and studying competitors to see how others run a pre-order model.

The biggest obstacle was the business’s reluctance to hold stock, given the high value of the medications and the risk of inventory sitting unsold. Several options were on the table, such as direct ordering without consultation, or without a deposit. Each was weighed against what operations could realistically run, until the team agreed on a workable flow:

View the product page → fill in the form → pharmacist consultation call → 100% deposit → source the medication and deliver

With the deposit paid up front, no stock is held before a customer has committed.

Three constraints leading to three design decisions

  • The medication is prescription-only and its price changes over time, so the product page shows no price. The pharmacist quotes the current price on the consultation call.
  • With no inventory, delivery depends on the supplier, so the page promises no firm delivery date. Customers are told the timing will come after the pharmacist’s callback, at two touchpoints: the guidance right above the registration form, and the success message after they submit.
  • Filling in a form and paying a deposit is a new way to buy, so the guidance sits right above the registration form on the product page, in a dropdown expanded by default. Customers see how pre-ordering works the moment they look up the product, and can collapse it to keep the page clean.

The result is a product page unlike any other medication page. It carries a registration form, and in place of a price and delivery time, it gives customers clear guidance and a detailed success message.

After go-live

I listened back to consultation calls and found that customers were caught off guard by the 100% deposit and upset that delivery took so long. I also noticed that callbacks were running late. At launch the page only had general guidance, so I added a deposit note and an expected delivery timeframe to complete the information. Lastly, I worked with the pharmacist team to streamline the callback process and shorten the wait for customers.

Lessons

  • Listening back to consultation calls is a rich source of insight for improving the UI and operations of a new feature.
  • Launch is not the end. A feature matures and delivers real value only when operations are proactively monitored and the teams involved keep improving the process together.

Results

In the first month, customers submitted registration forms but none went on to complete a deposit. From month two, orders began to climb once the deposit note, expected delivery timeframe and faster callbacks were in place. Customers likely also grew more comfortable with pre-ordering over time, and the channel has kept contributing steady, growing revenue since. Notably, the average order value is tens of times higher than a typical order on the platform.

On the operations side, callback response time dropped from one week to about 24 hours, with the fastest cases answered within 2 hours against a 48-hour target.
